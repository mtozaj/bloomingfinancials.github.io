5 Tax Deductions Every Startup Should Know (California Edition)

Reduce your tax bill with founder-friendly write-offs, examples, and California call-outs, so more of your cash stays in the business.

Quick note: This guide is for general education. Tax rules change and your facts matter, so run major moves by a professional. If you’re in California, state rules often differ from federal.

1) Start-up & Organizational Costs

When an active business begins, you can generally deduct up to $5,000 of qualifying start-up costs and, for an eligible entity, up to $5,000 of organizational costs. Each $5,000 allowance is reduced dollar for dollar when costs in that category exceed $50,000. Remaining eligible costs are generally amortized over 180 months, beginning in the month business starts. Equipment, acquisition costs, and costs of raising capital follow separate rules.

What usually qualifies

  • Pre-launch marketing and surveys
  • Travel to evaluate suppliers/customers
  • Attorney fees to draft formation documents
  • Eligible formation filing fees; classify recurring registered-agent charges separately
  • Accounting fees to set up books

What usually does not qualify

  • Assets and equipment (handled under depreciation/§179)
  • Interest, taxes, and research expenditures, which have their own deduction rules
  • Costs to acquire another business

Pro tip: Create two simple buckets in your bookkeeping: Start-up costs and Organizational costs. That makes the first-year deduction and the amortization entry straightforward.

California call-out

California generally follows the start-up and organizational-cost framework, but entity taxes are separate. The broad first-year $800 LLC tax exemption applied only to tax years beginning in 2021 through 2023. A new LLC formed in 2025 or 2026 should generally budget for the annual tax. Newly incorporated or qualified corporations can have a first-year minimum-tax exception, while tax on net income still applies. Narrow exceptions require a separate review.

Documentation checklist

  • Engagement letters and invoices (legal, accounting, marketing)
  • Receipts for state filing and registered agent fees
  • Travel logs (dates, purpose, who you met)
  • Business start date, costs by category, and any required tax-election records

2) Home Office Deduction

A qualifying self-employed home office generally must be used regularly and exclusively for business and meet a principal-place-of-business, client-meeting, or separate-structure test. The simplified method uses $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet; the actual-expense method allocates eligible housing costs. Income limits can restrict the deduction.

When it works well

  • Your home is your principal place of business
  • You regularly meet clients there; qualifying inventory storage has separate rules
  • You conduct administrative work there and have no other fixed location for substantial administrative work

Watch-outs

  • Exclusive use generally applies; qualifying inventory storage and daycare have exceptions
  • Ordinary W-2 employees generally cannot deduct home-office costs on their federal return; owner-employees need a separate reimbursement review
  • Repairs for the whole home are allocated; direct office repairs are 100%

California call-out

California generally follows the business-use-of-home eligibility rules, but depreciation and other state adjustments can differ. California also permits some eligible unreimbursed employee expenses as itemized deductions, subject to the 2%-of-AGI floor and other requirements. A federal restriction does not automatically settle the California result.

Quick example (actual-expense method)

200 sq ft office ÷ 1,000 sq ft home = 20% business use. Applying that percentage to $18,000 of eligible indirect costs gives $3,600 before deduction limits. Eligible office-only repairs are considered separately; improvements may need depreciation.

3) Equipment & Software (Section 179, Bonus Depreciation & De Minimis Safe Harbor)

Founders often buy laptops, servers, machinery, tools, and subscriptions in year one. The tax code gives multiple ways to expense these:

  • Section 179 expensing: Qualifying equipment and off-the-shelf software may be expensed when placed in service. Annual purchase limits, the taxable-business-income limit, business-use requirements, and special vehicle limits can restrict the deduction.
  • Bonus depreciation: Federal law restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Earlier acquisitions can remain subject to the previous phase-down rules. Eligibility, binding-contract dates, vehicle limits, and available elections matter.
  • De minimis safe harbor: Businesses without an applicable financial statement may qualify for a $2,500-or-less limit per invoice or separately substantiated item. Consistent book treatment, an accounting policy in place at the start of the year, and an annual election on a timely filed original return are required. It does not cover inventory or land.

Placed-in-service matters

Depreciation and Section 179 depend on when property is ready and available for its intended business use, not simply when you pay or first use it. Equipment ordered in December but not ready until January generally starts depreciation in January’s tax year.

California call-out

California does not conform to federal bonus depreciation and has a much lower Section 179 limit than federal law. Practically, you may get a full write-off on your federal return but a slower write-off on your California return. Plan for the state-tax cash impact, especially in equipment-heavy startups.

Documentation checklist

  • Invoices showing item description and cost
  • Proof of payment and delivery/installation date
  • Capitalization policy (for de minimis safe harbor)
  • Fixed-asset register tracking serial numbers and locations

4) Research & Development (R&D) Credit

Developing a product or writing software does not automatically qualify for the R&D credit. The work must meet the qualified-research tests, including technological uncertainty and a process of experimentation aimed at a permitted improvement. Routine maintenance, adaptation, and some funded or foreign research do not qualify.

What activities often qualify

  • Software engineering and prototyping
  • Hardware design and bench testing
  • Experimental iterations to improve performance or reliability
  • Formulation work in biotech, ag-tech, or materials

Costs that can count

  • W-2 wages for qualified technical staff
  • Contractor expenses (a portion may qualify)
  • Supplies consumed in experiments
  • Certain computer-use costs for qualified research; ordinary cloud subscriptions do not automatically qualify

Claim the credit on Form 6765. A qualified small business may elect up to $500,000 of the credit annually against eligible employer payroll taxes, subject to gross-receipts, business-age, and other limits. The election generally goes on a timely filed income-tax return; Form 8974 is used with the eligible payroll return. Keep project, wage, and experimentation records.

California call-out

California has a separate, generally nonrefundable research credit for qualifying California research, with its own calculation and limitations. It does not provide the federal payroll-tax offset. Separately, federal Section 174A generally allows current deductions for domestic research expenditures in tax years beginning after 2024; foreign research generally remains subject to 15-year amortization. Expense deductions and research credits are different benefits, and California calculations must be reviewed separately.

Field-tested tip

Stand up a lightweight “R&D memo” each quarter: goals, uncertainties, iterations, who worked on what, and links to tickets/PRs. It makes credit substantiation far smoother if examined.

5) Health Insurance Premiums (Self-Employed)

Self-employed owners can often deduct up to 100% of health, dental, and qualifying long-term-care premiums paid for themselves, a spouse, and dependents, even if you don’t itemize. The deduction reduces adjusted gross income.

How ownership type affects it

  • Sole proprietors and eligible disregarded LLC owners: The deduction is limited by adjusted earned income from the business under which the plan is established.
  • Partners in partnerships and LLCs taxed as partnerships: Coordinate partnership payment or reimbursement, K-1 reporting, and the owner-level deduction.
  • S-corp shareholders owning more than 2%: The corporation must pay or reimburse eligible premiums and include them in W-2 Box 1. The owner-level deduction depends on the remaining eligibility rules; a personally paid premium with no corporate reimbursement is not enough.

Common pitfalls

  • No deduction for months you were eligible for subsidized employer coverage through yourself, your spouse, a dependent, or a child under age 27, even if you declined it; the business earned-income limit also applies
  • Coordination needed if you also receive a premium tax credit
  • Qualified long-term-care premiums have age-based limits; document the plan, payments, and any required S-corp reimbursement

California call-out

California generally follows the federal self-employed health-insurance deduction. However, California does not allow a deduction for Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions (even though federal law does), so plan accordingly if you use HSAs.

Bonus Write-offs Worth a Look

  • Business mileage: Keep dated mileage and business-purpose records for eligible trips. Ordinary commuting and personal errands are not business mileage.
  • Education: Courses and professional books that maintain or improve skills used in the business.
  • Business meals (generally 50%): Save the receipt and note who and what business purpose.
  • Advertising and websites: Separate current operating costs from pre-opening start-up costs, acquired assets, and development costs that may need capitalization.
  • Software subscriptions: Project management, cloud, design tools; document business use.
  • Interest & bank fees: On business accounts and cards used for operations.

California Taxes Every Startup Should Plan For

  • Minimum franchise / annual tax: Budget for applicable $800 entity taxes. The general first-year LLC exemption ended after 2023; qualifying new corporations have a different minimum-tax exception.
  • LLC fee: LLCs taxed as partnerships or disregarded entities generally owe an additional fee when California-attributable total income reaches $250,000. The measure is generally gross income plus cost of goods sold, not net profit.
  • S-corporation tax: The general California rate is 1.5% of California taxable net income, with an $800 minimum where applicable. The minimum is a floor, not an extra $800 added to the calculated tax. Shareholders also report pass-through income.
  • C-corporation tax: California imposes a franchise tax rate on C-corps (with a minimum tax). Factor this into burn-rate planning.
  • Local taxes: Check city business-registration, license-tax, and gross-receipts-tax requirements where the business operates.
  • State conformity differences: California does not conform to the federal 20% pass-through deduction (IRC §199A), to federal bonus depreciation, or to HSA deductions. Your state bill can differ meaningfully from your federal one.

Founder’s Year-End Checklist

  • Reconcile books; categorize start-up vs. operating vs. fixed-asset purchases
  • Run an equipment plan: Section 179 vs. regular depreciation vs. bonus (federal) and California impact
  • Capture home-office square footage and utility totals
  • Keep contemporaneous mileage records; review missing records against supporting evidence rather than estimating unsupported trips
  • Inventory R&D projects; gather time sheets and experiment notes
  • Confirm owner health-insurance setup (especially >2% S-corp shareholders)
  • Estimate California state taxes and city taxes; calendar due dates

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to choose between Section 179 and bonus depreciation?

You can mix them. Many founders expense some assets under §179 and use bonus/regular depreciation for the rest. The optimal mix depends on profits, future tax rates, and California non-conformity.

Can I take the home-office deduction if I sometimes work at a coworking space?

Possibly. Using a coworking space does not by itself disqualify a home office. The home space must meet a qualifying-use test and generally be used regularly and exclusively for business. Administrative work, client meetings, and separate structures have specific rules.

We’re a pre-revenue tech startup. Is the R&D credit still worth it?

Often, yes. The federal credit can offset employer payroll taxes for eligible small businesses, producing real cash savings before profitability. Keep strong documentation from day one.

Are meals during fundraising trips deductible?

Not automatically. Ordinary business-travel meals are generally subject to a 50% limit, but costs connected with raising capital can have different treatment. Review the purpose of the trip before claiming a current deduction; entertainment is generally nondeductible.
